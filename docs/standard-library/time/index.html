Time

Also known as: clock date datetime calendar timezone time zone tz iana utc offset daylight saving dst sleep timer interval cron duration parse iso 8601 rfc 3339 timestamp from string

● Intermediate8 minBefore this: For LoopSpec 14.4

std.time tells the time, measures how long something takes, waits, repeats work at an interval, works with dates and time zones, and reads and writes times as text.

In simple terms

A watch, a stopwatch and a calendar in one: read the time, measure how long a job took, wait a moment, work out the date a month from now, see what the clocks show in another city, or read a date and time out of a line of text.

  • time.now_ms() is the time of day, in milliseconds since 1970. The clock can be changed, so don't measure with it.
  • time.monotonic_ms() never goes back: subtract two readings to measure a duration.

Every few moments

Each round starts 100 ms after the one before. A round that takes longer makes the next start at once: rounds are never skipped or doubled.

Reading the clock needs no capability: it changes nothing outside the program. Waiting, with sleep, every_ms or a timer, needs Clock. In the tests kavach test writes for you, a fake clock makes reading the time, sleep, after and each round of every instant and the same every time. A test block sets one with time.freeze, below.

When the clock changes

  • Waits (sleep, every, after, timeouts) and monotonic_ms use the system's clock that never jumps, as Rust, Go, Java and Python do: changing the clock or the time zone doesn't move them.
  • Whether time the machine slept counts is the system's own rule: it doesn't on Linux and macOS, and it does on Windows.
  • There is no wait until a time of day, and no cron schedule. A job at 09:00 every day belongs to the system's scheduler (cron, systemd timers, Task Scheduler), which starts the program.

Dates

TypeIs
DateA day of the calendar, with no time and no zone: year, month (1 to 12), day (1 to the month's last)

WeekdayMonday to Sunday
  • Months are 1 to 12, never from 0 as in JavaScript.
  • A month too short for the day gives its last day: January 31 and one month is February 29 in 2024, a leap year, and February 28 in 2026.
  • Every year uses the Gregorian calendar: 1900 is not a leap year, 2000 is.
  • A date that can't exist is refused. parse_date fails with Invalid, and making one stops the program:

A DateTime has no time zone: it is what a calendar and a wall clock show. A moment, milliseconds since 1970 as now_ms gives it, is another thing, so the two can't be mixed by accident. time.utc(ms) turns a moment into its DateTime in UTC, and dt.utc_ms() back. A named time zone, below, does the same for any place.

Time zones

A Zone is a named time zone, such as Asia/Kolkata or America/New_York: what the clocks there show at each moment, daylight saving time included. Like reading the clock, it needs no capability. One moment, in three places:

Function or methodGives
time.zone(name)The Zone named name, or the fixed offset +05:30; fails with UnknownZone(name) for a name the database doesn't have and for an offset that can't be (+24:00, +5:30)
z.at(ms)The DateTime the clocks in z show at the moment ms
z.moment(dt)The moment the clocks in z show dt; a skipped or repeated time is resolved as below
z.resolve(dt)Which moments show dt: Exact(ms), Skipped(earlier, later) or Repeated(earlier, later), a Local
z.offset_seconds(ms)How far z is ahead of UTC at the moment ms, in seconds (negative west of Greenwich)

time.zone_offset(name, ms)The same for a named zone of the database, none for any other name: what the others are built on

When daylight saving time starts, the clocks skip an hour, so some times of day never happen there. z.moment(dt) moves a skipped time later by the gap, as iCalendar, Java and JavaScript's Temporal do. To choose otherwise, match on z.resolve(dt):

When it ends, the clocks go back, so some times happen twice. z.moment(dt) takes the first:

  • Kavach never reads the machine's own time zone. A program names the zone it means, so it runs the same on every machine.
  • The zone rules come from the operating system on Linux and macOS, and from a copy built into the program on Windows.
  • An unknown name fails with UnknownZone, and time.zone_offset gives none:

A fixed offset

A Zone can also be a fixed offset from UTC, such as +05:30, which never changes. z.iso(ms) writes a moment as the clocks in the zone show it, with the offset:

  • An offset of zero is written Z.
  • An offset has seconds only when it needs them, as some zones' oldest offsets do.
  • An offset that can't be fails with UnknownZone, like an unknown name.
  • z.at(ms) and z.iso(ms) take a moment within ±30_000_000_000_000_000 ms of 1970, as their contract says.

Times as text

time.parse_date and three more read the formats iso writes. A Time and a DateTime have no zone, so their text can't have one. A moment needs a zone, so its text must:

FunctionReadsGives
time.parse_time(text)09:05, 09:05:30, 09:05:30.250A Time
time.parse_datetime(text)2026-11-04T09:05:30, with a time as aboveA DateTime
time.parse_moment(text)2026-11-04T09:05:30Z, 2026-11-04T14:35:30+05:30A moment, in milliseconds

Each of them fails with Invalid(text) for anything else:

  • Numbers have exactly the digits shown, so 9:05 and 2026-11-4 fail, and so does a sign where a digit should be.
  • A fraction of a second has 1 to 9 digits. Kavach keeps milliseconds, so digits past the third are dropped, not rounded: .1239 is 123 ms.
  • A leap second (23:59:60) fails: a Time has no such second.
  • parse_moment reads back any moment z.iso wrote, seconds in the offset included.
  • Other formats, such as 4 Nov 2026, are not read: they are an open question.

The time in a contract

Reading the clock needs no capability, but a contract may not do it: its answer would change from run to run.
